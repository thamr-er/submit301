CPU Scheduling Algorithms

Priority Scheduling, non-preemptively.

The scheduler hands the CPU to whichever ready process has the highest priority — and then, once it starts, refuses to take it back until the process finishes on its own. Powerful when priorities reflect what really matters. Brutal when they don't.

/ 01

The idea.

Every process in the system is tagged with a priority number. In most textbooks (and in this demo), a lower number means a higher priority — priority 1 outranks priority 5. Some systems flip the convention; the toggle in the simulator lets you try both.

When the CPU becomes free, the scheduler looks at the ready queue — every process that has arrived but not yet finished — and picks the one with the best priority. Ties are broken by arrival order (FCFS).

Crucially, this is the non-preemptive variant. Once a process owns the CPU, it keeps it. Even if a top-priority job arrives one millisecond later, it waits in line until the current process voluntarily releases the CPU (either by terminating or by blocking on I/O).

Contrast this with the preemptive version, where the arrival of a higher-priority process immediately bumps the running one back into the ready queue.

  1. Start the clock at t = 0.
  2. Look at every process with arrival ≤ t that is not yet completed.
  3. If none have arrived yet → CPU is idle, fast-forward to the next arrival.
  4. Otherwise: pick the one with the highest priority.
    (Tie-break: earlier arrival, then lower PID.)
  5. Run it to completion. t = t + burst.
  6. Mark it done. Go back to step 2.
  7. Stop when every process has finished.
Convention used here: smaller priority number = higher priority. Priority 1 is the most important; priority 9 is the least.

What you gain & lose.

Strengths

  • Important work goes first. If priorities reflect real urgency, this delivers excellent service to the jobs that matter most.
  • Simple to implement. Just keep the ready queue sorted by priority; pick the head when the CPU frees up.
  • Lower context-switch overhead than preemptive variants — once a process runs, the kernel leaves it alone.
  • Predictable for the running process. No surprise eviction mid-execution.

Weaknesses

  • Starvation. A steady stream of high-priority arrivals can keep low-priority jobs waiting forever.
  • Poor response time for urgent jobs that arrive after a long, low-priority process has already started — they must wait it out.
  • Priority inversion if priorities aren't carefully assigned (a critical job blocked behind less critical ones).
  • Priorities are hard to set well. Static priorities don't adapt to changing workloads.

The classic problem: starvation.

Imagine a low-priority background job sitting in the ready queue while new high-priority requests keep streaming in. Each time the CPU frees up, the scheduler picks one of the newcomers. The background job never gets to run. The standard fix is aging — gradually boost the priority of any process that has been waiting too long, so eventually it bubbles to the top.
